Start with the smallest pieces the machine knows: its primitives. In pictures they're small strokes and shapes, but nothing here depends on that. A primitive could be a note, a word or a step: anything that can be somewhere. Here they're plain symbols:

Each primitive gets one column. Columns are only ever made for primitives. Everything bigger is built from them, as we'll see, so the number of columns stays small however much the machine learns.

A piece that isn't made of other pieces. At rest the machine finds these by comparison: what two things share becomes a piece of its own (chapter 1).

One per primitive: everything the machine holds about that piece, as cells.

Picture a column as a tower of flat grids stacked on top of each other. Each grid is a floor. Every floor is the same: a grid of cells, where each cell is a position, an offset from the grid's middle. A lit cell means "this piece, at this offset".

One column, for piece A. The outlined cell on each floor is its middle.

The floors differ only in what the offset is measured from:

  • Floor 0, the bottom: where this piece is, measured from the centre of attention.
  • Floor 1: where this piece sits inside a thing made of pieces, measured from that thing's own centre.
  • Floor 2: where this piece sits inside a thing made of things, measured from that bigger thing's centre.
  • …and one floor more for each level up.

The next sections meet the floors one at a time: floor 0 first, then floor 1, then floor 2.

Try: Point at a dotted phrase to light that floor. Drag the column to turn it, and use the settings under it.

One layer of a column: a grid of positions. Floor 0 holds where things are; each floor above holds a piece's place inside a thing of the next level up.

Start with the bottom floor. Floor 0 says where pieces are, measured from the centre of attention (marked +). Put an A on the field and one cell lights on floor 0 of A's column, at the same offset from the middle.

So position needs no extra machinery: where something is, is simply which cell is lit. Two of the same piece are two lit cells in the same column.

Try: Place an A, then another A elsewhere: two cells in A's column. Move one and watch its cell move. Each column only ever lights its own piece.

A thing is a few pieces in a fixed arrangement. It doesn't get a column. It lights cells on a higher floor of its pieces' columns, floor 1, each at the piece's offset from the thing's own centre.

This splits what from where. Floor 0 changes as the thing moves; floor 1 doesn't. The same thing anywhere lights the same floor-1 cells.

A thing doesn't need to keep all of its cells. Its assembly is the smallest set of its cells from which completion (section 6) brings the whole thing back. Nobody sets how big an assembly is; it depends on what else the machine knows.

The machine tries the smallest sets first, one cell, then two, and keeps the first set that rebuilds the thing exactly and nothing else:

Why not A and B for the hat? A and B in that arrangement also fit the cup and the arch equally well. When the evidence fits two things equally, completion takes neither. So the hat's assembly needs the piece only the hat has. An assembly keeps what's distinctive.

A thing held as a few cells across its pieces' columns: the fewest from which completion rebuilds the whole.

Give the machine a few cells and it brings back what they belong to:

  1. Each lit cell votes for every thing it could be part of, centred wherever that would put it.
  2. A thing is accepted only if it describes two or more of the cells as one. Describing them with one placement is cheaper than describing them one by one (the same rule as in chapter 1).
  3. The biggest saving goes first. On a tie, the thing that claims least it hasn't seen goes first. If the evidence fits two things equally, neither is taken.
  4. The missing cells are filled in, and it repeats until nothing more saves (it settles).

Things can be made of things. The lamp is a hat above a cup. It's one level higher, so it lights cells one floor higher, floor 2, in the same primitive columns: still no new columns. A scene would be another level again.

The lamp

Its pieces' columns, three floors

Here are the lamp's columns in 3D. In A's column, on floor 1 are the hat's A and the cup's A, each measured from its own thing's centre. On floor 2 are the lamp's two As, measured from the lamp's centre, and on floor 0 is where they are.

Solid lines go up a column (the same piece, floor to floor); dashed lines join a thing's cells across columns. Dashed cubes on floor 2 are the lamp's cells outside its assembly, the ones completion fills in. Click a cube to keep only it and its neighbours; the cube in the corner snaps to a side.

Its assembly is just two As, one above the other. No single piece of the lamp is distinctive, since every piece also belongs to the hat, the cup or the arch. But that arrangement belongs to the lamp alone. And from C and D (section 6), completion brings back the whole lamp: six cells from two, one from each half.

This is the seed of the "episodic memory" idea: a few distinctive cells, spread across the levels of a scene, bringing the whole scene back.

Three things the engine learnt from pictures, each seen two ways at once: its cells in its pieces' columns, and the picture it stands for. Pick a thing, and point at a part in either view: it lights in both. Use Focus to look at a thing inside it (the corner inside "corner + bar", the block inside the twins). Offsets draws each piece's offset from the centre of the thing in focus; Kept or filled in shows which cells the assembly keeps and which completion fills in; Play completion shows it happening.

A snapshot of what the engine learnt (data/assemblies.js), not live yet.

  • Columns only for primitives, so there are few of them, whatever is learnt.
  • Cells are positions. Floor 0 is where; each higher floor is a piece's place inside a thing of that level.
  • What and where are separate: the same thing anywhere lights the same higher-floor cells.
  • An assembly is the smallest cue that completion turns back into the whole thing, so it keeps what's distinctive, and its size depends on what else is known.
  • Completion uses the same rule as everything else (describing several things as one saves), and refuses to guess when the evidence can't decide.

Open questions (your call):

  • Graded positions: a cell one step away shares nothing with its neighbour. Should nearby positions overlap?
  • One floor per level, or one shared floor (decision 004).
  • An abstract region where nearness comes from shared meaning rather than position.
