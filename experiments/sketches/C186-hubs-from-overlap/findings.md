# C186 · Hubs from overlap: findings

Run: one arm-seed at a time, `ARM='hubs by relation (seed N' node heat.js > out-hubsbyrelationseedN.txt` (and the same
for position), about three minutes each. Read [the hypothesis](hypothesis.md) first. One arm-seed, hubs by relation seed 1,
was seen before the predictions were written, and that's disclosed there.

## In short

**Hubs made of shared relations learned the second row in all three seeds,** 200 of 200 new second rows from the start, in
355, 287 and 297 runs. Without hubs (C185) it was 1 of 3. **Hubs made of shared positions** were no better than none: 1 of 3
(498, 449 and 429 runs). Times one digit was easy everywhere (11 to 15 runs).

**What the relation hubs were.** Comparing the seven kept ways of working (six equal versions of adding, and times one
digit) in pairs, and then their overlaps in turn, formed 17 hubs. The most widely shared relations, in all seven, were
how the parts sit relative to each other: the sign just below the answer, the carry one row up and one left of where the
answer goes, the sign written along beside it. In driving terms, those are the "more load, more push" of these lessons:
not where things are, but how they go together.

**What completion did.** When one part moved, the parts related to it snapped along by the most widely shared relation
between them, and that was tried as one change. On the second worked example, the first changes that cooled more were
completions: moving the hold carried the carry read, the answer, the carry write and the sign write with it; moving the top
read carried the hold, the carry read and the sign. Within about 40 attempts the page was near settled, and it settled by
attempt 72 to 74 in every seed. Without hubs, that example stuck in every seed. On the third example the hubs (now 25,
including the new way of working) carried it in 4 to 6 attempts to most of the way, and it settled in about 60 runs. On the
fourth, an offer settled at once.

**Why positions didn't help.** A hub of shared positions can only say "keep this part where it is". It can't say where a
part should go when another moves. That's the owner's distinction made concrete: abstraction that can transfer is
relational.

## Disclosed, and a caveat

- **One arm-seed seen before the predictions** (in the hypothesis).
- **A coincidence was settled on the way, in every relation seed.** The way of working that settled the second worked
  example held a digit of the first row instead of the multiplier, and wrote the sign in odd places (a coincidence that
  fitted that page). The third worked example settled the right one, and by test the right one was what the start brought
  up (200 of 200). The coincidence was learned and then outgrown, as in C178. One example can still mislead.
- Comparing all pairs at the start of adapting stands in for the owner's continuous background comparing. The overlap isn't
  sparsely sampled in this taste.

## The ledger

As C185, with hubs from overlap as structure in the substrate. **The code's:**
- comparing the pairs (when);
- the use rules: completion by the most widely shared relation, and holding shared positions;
- the search's ordering;
- practising, blame, and inclusion.

## What it shows

Hubs over experiences, built on what's already there with no second volume, give the substrate relational abstraction,
and that abstraction turns part of the search into completion. Moving one piece brings its related pieces along, so a
pair that search couldn't find comes as one change. It's the owner's picture: schema emerging from the overlap of spatial
experiences, and connecting them. Shared positions don't do this. Shared relations do.
