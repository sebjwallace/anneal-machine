# How heat restructures

> **Superseded in part by [nothing breaks](nothing-breaks.md).** §7–§9 below conclude that some breaking is needed, at the contact points, once one side has settled. A later exchange concluded that bonds never break: mistakes are outcompeted and sink to cold storage. This note is kept as it was, as the record.

A theory note, from a long question-and-answer exchange between the owner and the assistant. The assistant asked a
question, the owner answered, the assistant responded and asked the next. It ran until both were on the same page.
**Nothing here has been tested.** It sets out what the machine does when it detects the kind of heat that changes
structure: what it forms, what it breaks, when, and what it does with heat it can't settle.

It builds on [structure over strength](structure-over-strength.md), which says reinforcement means more connections
rather than stronger ones. That note leaves a gap this one fills: without strengths, what protects the machine from
overreacting? Related: [feelings as heat](feelings-as-heat.md) (curiosity as outward heat, rationalisation as covering
heat), [flow and heat](flow-and-heat.md), [learned computation](learned-computation.md) (the language).

**On vocabulary.** The owner's direction is to borrow words from set theory where they fit: union, intersection,
difference, subset. Nothing is borrowed from other parts of mathematics, to keep the language focused and pure. Heat
and energy stay the project's own ideas, sitting on top of the set words. Where this note uses set words, they mean
exactly their set meanings.

---

## 1. The problem this note answers

In a machine with strengths, one odd experience can only do a little damage. It nudges a number slightly, and the
next ordinary experience nudges it back. Noise washes out because nothing changes much at once. The slowness is the
protection.

This machine has no numbers on its bonds. A bond is there or it isn't. So when heat says "this doesn't fit", the
reaction is not a nudge but a real change: a bond formed or broken outright. That is what makes it fast and clean.
It also means one fluke could, unprotected, cut a good memory in half, or bond two things that only happened to turn
up together once. The protection has to come from somewhere other than strength. This note's answer is that it
comes from **completion, context, competition and timing**: from how structures meet, not from any setting on a bond.

## 2. Heat as two set differences

At any moment two sets matter: **what is active** (what the world is showing now), and **what the structure accounts
for** (what the existing bonds and assemblies hold, bring back or expect). When they match, there is no heat and
nothing changes. Heat is what is left over when one is taken from the other, and the two directions of that
subtraction give the two kinds of heat that change structure:

- **Active minus accounted for: a gap.** Members are on, but nothing holds them. This is something new, not
  something wrong. The response is to **form**: bond the uncovered members into an assembly, bonded to the cool,
  held context they arrived with. That empties the difference. Next time it comes out empty, so there is no heat.
- **Expected minus active: a misfit.** An assembly is brought back and says "these members should be here", and some
  aren't. What happens next is the subject of most of this note, and it is not the simple cut first assumed (§5).

So the general shape of cooling is: **change the structure until the differences are empty.** Heat has a size, which
is just how many members are in those leftover sets. No averaging is needed anywhere.

There is a second family of heat (the owner has mentioned it but not yet described it). This note is only about the
heat that reconfigures, the bonding side.

## 3. Small gaps are filled from inside

**The example.** The machine has learnt "cat" well. It sees "cxt", a typo: the expected "a" is missing and an
unexpected "x" has arrived. Unprotected, the machine would take the intersection of expected and arrived, "c" and
"t", make that the core, and split off "a" and "x" as interchangeable parts. One typo would turn a well-learnt word
into a template it never was.

**What actually happens (the owner's answer).** The inside overrules the input. Completion brings back the "a" from
memory, and brought-back members already count as evidence in the core. The "x" is left on its own: noticed, a
little hot, but with nothing to attach to. You might not even notice the typo. If you do, given everything around
it, you ignore it and move on.

**Two levels of heat.** This shows there is heat that is *noticed* and heat that *changes structure*. Small heat
inside a coherent structure is noticed at most, and then ignored.

**What "small" means, concretely.** Completion only fills in when enough of an assembly has actually arrived to
open its gates. In set terms: the part of the assembly that arrived covers enough of it. A small gap is one where
most of a whole is present and only a few members are missing. A huge gap is one where too little has arrived for
any gate to open, so nothing is filled in.

## 4. Context decides whether completion wins

**The example.** "cot" is a real word the machine hasn't learnt. On the surface it looks exactly like the typo:
"c" and "t" present, "a" missing, something unexpected in the middle. What should be different?

**The owner's answer: context.** In a paragraph about animals, the surrounding held assemblies all back "cat".
Completion has plenty to lean on, fills in the "a", and the heat is cooled straight away. "cot" is read as "cat",
exactly like the typo. In a paragraph about a nursery, a baby and sleeping, nothing around backs "cat". Completion
has only "c" and "t" to work from and can't settle. The heat stays.

**The rule this gives.** Heat that completion cools is treated as noise. Heat that survives is the kind that should
change structure. The guard against noise is not a property of the bond. It is whether completion has enough support
from the context.

"Context" here means the held surrounding assemblies, not context carried in cells, so this is consistent with
[decision 009](../decisions/009-no-context-in-cells.md).

**The crosstalk case.** Where a cat and a baby are both sleeping in a cot, and the machine doesn't know "cot", it
will read "cot" as "cat" until "cot" is learnt in its own context. Until you know what a word means, you interpret it
as something else.

**A consequence worth keeping.** If "cot" is corrected every time it appears in a cat-ish context, the heat is
cooled each time and nothing forms. The machine can only learn "cot" from occasions where the context doesn't back
"cat". **Learning depends on meeting the thing where it is surprising.**

## 5. Form beside; don't cut

The first picture had misfit heat triggering the cut on "cat". In the owner's version, "cat" is never cut. Learning
"cot" means forming a new assembly *beside* "cat": the "o" together with the nursery context it arrived in. "cat"
stays exactly as it was. The two are told apart by which context backs each.

This is structure over strength again: the new is added, the old is left alone.

**Abstraction becomes forming too.** If nothing is split, the shared core of two experiences can't be made by
cutting one of them. It has to be **formed as a new assembly from their intersection**, with the originals left
intact. So the cut changes from "split what's there" to "form what's shared". This is a real change to how the
theory described the cut, and both parties judged it a better one.

## 6. Being wrong is settled by competition

**The question.** If new things are formed beside old ones and noise is cooled by completion, does the machine ever
need to break anything? What about when the old memory itself is wrong?

**The owner's answer.** Being wrong is not forgetting. You can remember the times you were wrong, and what led you
there. Some times you thought you were wrong, it turned out you were right. Correction is continuous learning:
continuous structure, continuous support.

When an understanding fits and settles but is wrong, new structure comes in that competes with it. Where the two
claim the same ground they compete, and competition already works on "most support wins". As the new structure
picks up more context and more connections, it starts winning. The old one doesn't vanish, it stops being what
completes. This is uncomfortable, which is partly why people avoid learning things that show them they were wrong.
Over time the new can become more coherent than the old, and one day, without noticing, you simply understand it
differently.

## 7. But structure has a cost, so some breaking is needed

**The slip that showed it.** Partway through answering, the owner began using words like "loosens", "unlodges" and
"unsettles": heat doing something to the old structure itself. He started by saying nothing is lost and ended up
describing heat breaking things away under the weight of competition. He noted that this drift in his own thinking
might be the very thing he was describing: two ideas competing in him as he spoke. (He also noted that something as
short-lived as that is more likely carried by latches than by bonds.)

**The pushback that prompted it.** In a binary machine there is no "weaker". Either a bond is there or it isn't, so
"loosening" is where strength could quietly slip back in. Has the old structure changed, or has it only stopped
winning?

**The owner's resolution: a trade-off.**

- **Never forgetting costs.** Structure is encapsulated heat, energy trapped in form. If nothing is ever let go,
  structure piles on structure. On a computer that is memory, and RAM most of all. Every kept rival is also one more
  thing in every competition, so crosstalk grows.
- **Forgetting costs too.** You lose the reason you were wrong the first time, so you can repeat the same mistake
  and reach the same false conclusions.

Neither is simply right. So the machine must break structure to some degree, and if you tallied its bonds you would
see the count trend upwards as it learns, going up and down along the way as it learns and forgets. It is looking for
a balance between the two.

## 8. Breaking is local, at the contact points

The owner's picture is metal under pressure. Rub two surfaces together hard enough and the points of contact heat
up. Under enough pressure the heat loosens the atoms, so the contact points can take a new shape: bonds breaking and
forming. When it cools, the contact points have moulded to fit each other better.

Applied here: when two structures compete, there is something between them that doesn't fit. That piece heats up,
and that piece is what breaks and reforms. It doesn't mean the old structure is wholly wrong. Something about it
didn't fit with the winner. Breaking happens **at the contact points only**. Everything away from them is untouched.

**What this buys.** It settles the forgetting trade-off better than either extreme. The old structure mostly
survives, so the old belief and what led to it can still be remembered. What's gone is only the piece that let it
compete for the contested ground. **You forget the claim, not the memory.**

**A vocabulary correction.** The owner first called the contact point "the intersection". In this language the
intersection is the shared part, and the shared part is what fits. For "cat" and "cot", the intersection is "c" and
"t", and nothing is wrong there. The heat is at the bonds running *out of* the shared part towards the rival pieces,
"a" for one and "o" for the other: where both structures lean on the shared part but expect different things of it.
So the contact point is not the intersection. Using one word for both would make it mean two opposite things.

## 9. Losing is not the same as being wrong

**The question.** If the structure with more support wins and the loser's contact bonds break, the machine will
sometimes destroy something true just because it is less popular. How can it tell "lost because wrong" from "lost
because less familiar" before it breaks anything?

**The owner's answer.** This is one of the hardest things the brain does, and it is what people do when they sit down
and think. It takes real energy: looking from different angles, considering what isn't obvious. Humans usually do it
for reasons of emotional heat (fear of an outcome, unhappiness) or for curiosity and fun. Otherwise they are
complacent and drift.

A true idea that lost may still be coherent, but **hard to get to**: its completion lies further away. The winner
felt right because its completion was nearer, so it felt more obvious. But the right thing is not always the obvious
thing. Sometimes completion needs another way in, through another set of assemblies, which means looking from a
particular point of view. As more structure is built that gets there quicker, it becomes more obvious, and eventually
it overrules the other, because getting to it has become easier.

Comparing two competing ideas is itself how that happens. You weigh them against someone's perspective, a priority,
anything. Attention jumps around and **forms assemblies that weren't there before**. As structure builds out around
them, energy flows more towards one than the other, and the balance swings back and forth until one becomes so
coherent and simple that you go with it, or you give up. It is messy. The owner pointed out that this exchange was an
example: each question made him build a little more structure, and the balance between his answers swung.

**What follows.**

- The machine can't tell wrong from unfamiliar *in the moment of the contest*. In that moment it only knows which
  side is easier to get to. Being right shows up over time: a true idea keeps fitting as structure is built around
  it from new angles, and a wrong one keeps running into heat.
- "Most support wins" becomes more honest under this reading: support means how many ways in a thing has, and the
  true one tends to gather more ways in as it is examined.
- **Timing is the protection.** If breaking happened the moment one side pulled ahead, the machine would throw away
  the true-but-harder-to-reach idea before exploration found the way in. So **while two structures are still
  contesting, both are kept and the tie is held** (ties are already held in the core). **Breaking happens only once
  one side has settled**: when the heat at the contact point has gone because one side fits from many angles and the
  other no longer gets a look in. This is the protection §1 asked for. It isn't a strength. It's not breaking until
  the contest is over.

## 10. When a contest never settles

**The question.** However much the machine explores, the two stay roughly even and the contact point stays hot. What
then?

**The owner's answer.** At this point you have hit the limits of the structure inside. There are three things
people do:

1. **Look outward.** Research, ask questions: find structure in the world and bring it in, because the structure
   inside isn't enough. Science is the stereotype: it looks at nature to find the structure and bring it into the
   mind. Sometimes the structure can't be obtained, and people struggle with it.
2. **Make up a fiction.** As heat builds and lasts, it is uncomfortable, and a story that closes the gap settles it.
   You can see why people do this. **The machine must not.** It may be fine for humans; it isn't practically useful
   here.
3. **Let it go.** The contemplative traditions, Buddhist and Taoist, don't necessarily seek resolution. They settle
   on it being all right for things to be unsettled, and allow paradox and contradiction to be. The heat
   evaporates. The machine will have to do this at some point (an exhaust, as in the earlier notes), or it gets
   hung up on something when it could be exploring very different things. And answers are often found by looking
   somewhere completely different, as science and mathematics have shown time and again.

Letting go has a further consequence the owner drew out. When heat inside settles, attention is free for heat
elsewhere: heat trapped in other people and other animals, as suffering. The owner wondered whether the compassion
some Buddhists practise is partly a consequence of being able to let go. Venting one's own heat makes room to attend
to heat outside.

**A fourth option: contain it.** Humans struggle when they suppress heat over the long term, but the machine might
contain it: park the contest, still hot but sealed. If new structure later leads back to it from a different angle,
the heat finally has a pathway out, and it releases all at once and drains quickly, because the structure now gives
it somewhere to go. That is the moment of sudden understanding.

**So the machine's honest options** are to look outward, to let go, and to contain. Making things up is not one of
them.

**A refinement (assistant's, agreed in substance).** In a binary machine, letting go and containing may be the same
thing structurally. If the heat is vented but no bonds are broken, both rivals are still there, untouched. They just
stop drawing attention, and that *is* containment. The real distinction is between **letting go of the heat** (always
needed, so the machine can move on) and **letting go of the structure** (optional, done for cost: the forgetting
trade-off of §7).

## 11. Fiction can creep in through completion

**The worry.** The machine can't rule out fiction just by deciding not to do it, because it already fills in from
inside: reading "cxt" as "cat" put an "a" there that never arrived. That is a tiny, useful fiction. If members the
machine brought back itself can take part in forming new bonds, it will build structure out of its own guesses.

**The owner's first answer: structure is what cooling leaves behind.** Heat shakes things loose. Whatever lets the
energy flow again and drop is what stays. What is kept is decided by the result, not by a rule about where things
came from.

**The pushback.** That alone doesn't keep fiction out. Fiction is exactly something that cools without the world:
people make it up because it settles the heat. Worse, once a fiction has become structure, it fills in what arrives,
so the world may never get to contradict it. It seals itself.

**The owner's fuller answer: coherence decides how much the inside can impose.** It is again which one wins, and
how coherent the inside is decides it.

- **Strong, coherent inside, small gaps.** As with "cat", the inside fills the gaps, because the gaps are only a
  little heat.
- **Weak, sparse inside, huge gaps.** The owner's example is joining a new team on a new project, hearing acronyms
  you don't know. Your structure is sparse patches with gaps everywhere between them. You feel lost and confused.
  What you can impose is loose and itself full of heat, because things don't link up, so it can't settle and can't
  win. The only way to cool is to take structure in from outside, if you are inclined to resolve it, until the heat
  gradually drops. That is learning.

**What this buys.** A weak inside can't easily produce fiction, because it doesn't settle anything. The guard comes
from the dynamics, not a rule.

**What it leaves open.** Coherence is what earns the right to impose, and **a coherent but wrong structure has that
right too**. The cat-and-baby-in-a-cot reading is exactly this: confident, small gap, filled in, wrong, and nothing in
that moment produces the heat that would let the world correct it.

## 12. Confident mistakes are corrected by variety

**The question.** If a well-connected wrong structure keeps filling small gaps confidently, where does the heat come
from that eventually exposes it?

**The owner's answer.** Curiosity, exploration and drift. If you impose on small details you won't notice, and as far
as you're concerned all is well. But later you meet a situation with some overlap with the old one, coming at it
from a different angle. You pull in new structures that connect with the old one, and now they fill the gaps
differently. Before you knew what a cot was, you corrected "cot" to "cat". Then you have a baby, you have a cot, you
build up rich experience, and you will never confuse them again, because the structure built out now separates them.

**The machine doesn't know it is wrong.** It can't, unless it chases every little bit of heat, and then it goes down
rabbit holes and gets distracted. To solve a big problem it has to stay focused, and little heat is a distraction that
builds up. As with letting go, lowering heat inside frees attention for heat elsewhere. That is exploration.

**Large gaps draw it out.** As the earlier notes have it, curiosity goes to large gaps: a prediction or an imagined
thing you want to fill in with reality, or the leading edge of something where you don't know where it goes. That
draws you out, and the owner holds that this is the only way learning actually happens.

**In a nutshell:** you can be oblivious to how wrong you are until you learn something new by happenstance. The more
variety, the more you realise you were wrong. **For the machine, exploration isn't optional. It is the only route by
which its own confident mistakes ever get corrected.**

## 13. Two trade-offs without a true answer

Both are probably settings to be chosen rather than solved, the way forgetting is.

**Chase small heat now, or park it.** Chase every small heat and the machine never focuses. Ignore it all and it stays
confidently wrong. How much heat is worth chasing, and when to let go, is not pinned down.

**Forget, or keep.** The owner's proposal goes most of the way. Forgetting doesn't have to mean deleting structure.
It can mean **moving it somewhere else**. Keep everything, compressed, on disk, where there is room for a huge
amount. As structure is warmed or primed, stream it into RAM. As it goes cold, move it back out to disk. Only what is
relevant sits in memory. This doesn't fully solve the trade-off, but it gets more value out of it than a flat choice,
and the technology for this pattern is well developed.

Points worth recording about the proposal:

- **It may solve crosstalk as well as memory.** If cold structure on disk takes no part in completion and
  competition, it stops crowding every contest. That was the other cost of never forgetting (§7).
- **It fits containment.** A parked contest (§10) is cold structure on disk. If a new angle primes it, it is brought
  back, and the sudden release can happen. Forgotten, but not lost.
- **It needs a way in.** Something in RAM has to know what to warm. If priming can't reach a cold structure, it is
  effectively forgotten even though it still exists. Whatever does the reaching is itself structure with its own cost.
  How much of it there must be, and what it looks like, is open.

## 14. The whole picture in one pass

Heat is the gap between what's active and what the structure accounts for. Small gaps inside a coherent structure are
filled in by completion and ignored. That's useful, and it also means the machine can be confidently wrong without
noticing. Big gaps can't be filled from inside, so structure has to come in from the world, and that is learning. New
things are formed beside old ones rather than cutting them, and abstraction is forming the shared part, not splitting
the originals. When two structures claim the same ground they compete, and both are kept while the contest is hot.
Bonds are broken only at the contact points, and only once one side has settled, so being wrong mostly means losing
the contest rather than losing the memory. If a contest never settles, the machine looks outward for more structure,
or lets the heat go and parks the contest so a new angle can open it again. It never invents structure to close the
gap. It doesn't catch its own confident mistakes by checking itself. It catches them by meeting variety. Forgetting
need not be deleting: cold structure can move to disk and come back when primed.

## 15. Open questions and what would test them

1. **Noise is ignored.** Teach "cat" well, then show "cxt" in a cat-ish context. Does completion fill the "a", leave the
   "x" unattached, and change no bonds?
2. **Context separates.** Show "cot" in a context that doesn't back "cat". Does the heat survive, and does a new
   assembly form beside "cat" with "cat" untouched? Does "cot" in a cat-ish context still read as "cat"?
3. **Abstraction by forming.** Do two experiences that share a core produce a new assembly from their intersection,
   with both originals intact?
4. **Breaking waits.** Set two structures contesting. Are both kept while the contact point is hot, and are bonds
   broken only at the contact points, only after one side has settled? Is the rest of the loser still completable
   from its other ways in?
5. **What "settled" means as a set condition.** This has to be pinned down before breaking can be written in the
   language. It is not yet defined.
6. **No self-sealing.** Can a coherent wrong reading ever be corrected without the world producing a gap too big to
   fill? Test by giving the machine new angles on the same material and watching whether the wrong merger separates.
7. **Fill-ins and forming.** Should members the machine brought back itself be allowed to form bonds? The theory leans
   to "cooling decides", with coherence as the guard, but this is the most likely route for fiction and should be
   tested both ways.
8. **The tally.** Over a long run, does the bond count trend upwards with ups and downs, rather than growing without
   end or collapsing?
9. **The two trade-offs.** How much small heat to chase, and when to let go; and what in RAM decides which cold
   structure to warm from disk.
